# Skimming, Scanning, and Reading for Understanding

> **Core idea:** Effective readers change their reading strategy depending on their **purpose**. **Skimming** helps you get the general idea, **scanning** helps you find specific information, and **reading for understanding** requires careful reading to understand ideas, relationships, and meaning.

---

## 1. Why Use Different Reading Strategies?

You do not need to read every text in exactly the same way.

For example:

* Looking quickly through a newspaper → **Skimming**
* Finding a person's name in a passage → **Scanning**
* Studying a textbook for an examination → **Reading for understanding**

The Ethiopian FLEn 1011 course emphasizes adapting reading strategies to the purpose of reading.

> **Your purpose determines how you read.**

---

# 2. Skimming

**Skimming** means reading quickly to get the **general idea or gist** of a text.

You do not read every word.

### Look at:

* Title
* Headings
* Subheadings
* First sentences
* Last sentences
* Repeated words
* Important terms
* Pictures, graphs, or captions

### Example

You have a 10-page article about population growth.

You skim it because you want to know:

> **“What is this article mainly about?”**

You are not looking for a particular number or date.

### Skimming is used to:

* Find the main idea
* Get an overview
* Preview a text
* Decide whether a text is useful
* Understand the general topic

> **Skimming = General idea**

---

# 3. Scanning

**Scanning** means reading quickly to find **specific information**.

You already know what information you are looking for.

### You might scan for:

* Names
* Dates
* Numbers
* Places
* Definitions
* Specific facts
* Keywords
* Statistics

### Example

You are reading a passage about population and the question asks:

> **“What was the population in 2010?”**

You scan the text for:

> **2010**

You do not need to read the entire passage carefully.

> **Scanning = Specific information**

---

# 4. Skimming vs. Scanning

| Skimming                  | Scanning                     |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| General understanding     | Specific information         |
| Main idea                 | Particular detail            |
| Read quickly              | Search quickly               |
| Look at overall structure | Look for keywords            |
| “What is this about?”     | “Where is this information?” |

### Memory trick

> **SKIM = BIG picture**

> **SCAN = SPECIFIC information**

---

# 5. Reading for Understanding

**Reading for understanding** means reading carefully enough to understand the **meaning, ideas, relationships, and important details** of a text.

It goes deeper than skimming or scanning.

You should understand:

* Main ideas
* Supporting details
* Vocabulary in context
* Relationships between ideas
* Causes and effects
* Comparisons
* Examples
* Conclusions
* The writer's purpose

### Example

If you are studying a textbook chapter for an examination, you need more than the general idea.

You should be able to explain:

> What is the main idea?

> What are the important details?

> Why did something happen?

> What evidence supports the idea?

> What conclusion can be drawn?

---

# 6. Three Levels of Reading

Think of reading as three levels:

### Level 1 — Skimming

> **What is this text about?**

### Level 2 — Scanning

> **Where is the information I need?**

### Level 3 — Understanding

> **What does the text actually mean?**

### Simple sequence

> **Skim → Scan → Understand**

You may not always use all three, but knowing the difference helps you choose the right strategy.

---

# 7. Before Reading: Preview

Before reading carefully, quickly examine the text.

Look at:

* Title
* Headings
* Subheadings
* Images
* Charts
* Bold words
* First paragraph

Then ask:

> What do I already know about this topic?

> What do I expect to learn?

> Why am I reading this text?

This activates your **background knowledge** and gives you a purpose for reading.

---

# 8. Reading for Main Ideas

The **main idea** is the central message of a paragraph or passage.

Ask:

> **What is the writer mainly saying?**

### Example

> Regular exercise benefits university students. It can improve physical fitness, reduce stress, and help students sleep better.

### Main idea:

> **Regular exercise benefits university students.**

### Supporting details:

* Improves physical fitness
* Reduces stress
* Helps sleep

> **Main idea = central message**

> **Details = information supporting the message**

---

# 9. Reading for Specific Details

When looking for details, ask:

* Who?
* What?
* Where?
* When?
* Why?
* How?

### Example

> Zinedine Zidane was born in Marseille, France, in 1972.

If the question asks:

> **“Where was Zidane born?”**

Scan for the answer:

> **Marseille, France**

If the question asks:

> **“When was he born?”**

Scan for:

> **1972**

---

# 10. Reading for Meaning from Context

You will often encounter unfamiliar words.

Do not immediately stop reading.

Use **context clues**.

Look at:

* Words before the unknown word
* Words after it
* Examples
* Definitions
* Contrasts
* Causes and effects

### Example

> The area is **arid**. It receives very little rainfall and has a dry climate.

Even if you do not know **arid**, the context suggests:

> **arid = very dry**

---

# 11. Reading for Relationships

Good understanding means recognizing how ideas connect.

### Cause and effect

> Heavy rainfall caused flooding.

**Cause:** Heavy rainfall
**Effect:** Flooding

### Contrast

> Urban areas are densely populated, whereas rural areas are often less densely populated.

**Contrast:** Urban vs. rural

### Example

> Many factors affect population distribution. **For example**, climate and access to water influence where people live.

**Main idea:** Factors affect population distribution.

**Example:** Climate and water.

---

# 12. Reading for the Writer's Purpose

Ask:

> **Why did the writer write this text?**

Common purposes include:

### To inform

> Give facts or information.

### To explain

> Make an idea or process clear.

### To describe

> Give information about a person, place, object, or situation.

### To persuade

> Convince the reader to accept an idea.

### To entertain

> Provide enjoyment.

Understanding the purpose helps you understand the text more effectively.

---

# 13. Reading Actively

**Active reading** means interacting with the text instead of simply looking at the words.

While reading:

* Ask questions
* Underline key ideas
* Highlight important terms
* Make short notes
* Predict what comes next
* Connect new information to what you already know
* Check unfamiliar words
* Summarize sections

### Example questions

> What is the main idea?

> Why does this happen?

> What evidence does the writer give?

> What does this word mean here?

> What can I conclude?

> **Active reading → deeper understanding**

---

# 14. A Practical Reading Process

Use:

> **P → S → R → Q → S**

### **P — Preview**

Look at the title, headings, and structure.

### **S — Skim**

Get the general idea.

### **R — Read**

Read important sections carefully.

### **Q — Question**

Ask questions about the text.

### **S — Summarize**

Explain the main points in your own words.

This process is especially useful when studying academic texts.

# 15. Choosing the Right Strategy

| Your purpose                    | Best strategy                  |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Find the general topic          | **Skimming**                   |
| Find the main idea              | **Skimming**                   |
| Find a date                     | **Scanning**                   |
| Find a name                     | **Scanning**                   |
| Find a number                   | **Scanning**                   |
| Understand an argument          | **Reading for understanding**  |
| Study for an exam               | **Reading for understanding**  |
| Find a specific definition      | **Scanning + careful reading** |
| Decide whether a text is useful | **Skimming**                   |
| Understand causes and effects   | **Reading for understanding**  |

# 16. Common Exam Questions

### Main idea

> What is the passage mainly about?

→ **Skim for the overall message.**

### Specific information

> According to the passage, when did...?

→ **Scan for the date.**

### Meaning

> What does the word “X” mean in the passage?

→ **Read the surrounding context.**

### Inference

> What can be inferred from the passage?

→ **Understand the information and draw a logical conclusion.**

### Purpose

> Why did the author write the passage?

→ **Look at the overall message and language used.**

---

# 17. Common Mistakes

### ❌ Skimming every word

Skimming is supposed to be **quick**.

### ❌ Scanning without knowing what you want

Scanning works best when you have a **specific target**.

### ❌ Using scanning for deep understanding

Finding a number does not mean you understand the passage.

### ❌ Reading every text slowly

This wastes time when you only need general or specific information.

### ❌ Ignoring the purpose

Always ask:

> **Why am I reading this?**

---

# 18. Exam Strategy

Suppose you receive a long reading passage.

### Step 1 — Read the questions

Know what information you need.

### Step 2 — Skim the passage

Identify:

* Topic
* Main idea
* Structure

### Step 3 — Scan

Find:

* Names
* Dates
* Numbers
* Keywords

### Step 4 — Read carefully

For questions requiring:

* Meaning
* Inference
* Cause/effect
* Comparison
* Writer's purpose

### Step 5 — Check the evidence

Make sure your answer is supported by the passage.

> **Question → Strategy → Evidence → Answer**

---

## ⭐ Exam Focus

### Memorize these three:

> **Skimming = reading quickly for the general idea or gist.**

> **Scanning = reading quickly for specific information.**

> **Reading for understanding = reading carefully to understand meaning, ideas, details, and relationships.**

### Fast exam trick

If the question asks:

> **“What is the passage mainly about?”**

→ **SKIM**

If it asks:

> **“When/where/who/how many?”**

→ **SCAN**

If it asks:

> **“Why/how/what does the writer mean/what can be inferred?”**

→ **READ FOR UNDERSTANDING**
