# Reading for Main Ideas and Details

> **Core idea:** Good reading means understanding both the **main idea** of a text and the **details that support it**. The main idea tells you what the text is mainly about; details explain, prove, or develop that idea.

## 1. Main Idea

The **main idea** is the most important point the writer wants the reader to understand.

It answers:

> **What is the text mainly saying?**

### Example

> University students need good time-management skills. They have lectures to attend, assignments to complete, examinations to prepare for, and personal responsibilities to manage. Planning their time helps them complete these activities effectively.

### Main idea:

> **Good time management helps university students manage their responsibilities.**

The other information supports this central idea.

---

## 2. Topic vs. Main Idea

Do not confuse the **topic** with the **main idea**.

### Topic

The general subject.

> **Topic:** Time management

### Main idea

What the writer says about that subject.

> **Main idea:** Good time management helps students manage their academic and personal responsibilities.

### Easy memory

> **Topic = What?**

> **Main idea = What about it?**

---

## 3. Supporting Details

**Supporting details** are pieces of information that explain or support the main idea.

They may include:

* Facts
* Examples
* Reasons
* Explanations
* Descriptions
* Statistics
* Evidence

### Example

**Main idea:**

> Exercise benefits university students.

**Supporting details:**

* It improves physical fitness.
* It can reduce stress.
* It may improve concentration.
* It can help students sleep better.

> **Main idea = central message**
> **Details = evidence and explanation**

---

## 4. Finding the Main Idea

The main idea may appear in different places.

### A. At the beginning

The first sentence may introduce the central point.

> **Regular exercise is important for students.** It improves physical fitness and can reduce stress.

### B. At the end

The paragraph may provide details first and give the main point at the end.

> Students often face academic pressure, lack of sleep, and heavy workloads. **Therefore, managing stress is important for university students.**

### C. Throughout the paragraph

Sometimes the main idea is **not directly stated**.

You must combine the details and determine the central message.

This is called an **implied main idea**.

---

## 5. Topic Sentence

A **topic sentence** usually introduces the main idea of a paragraph.

It often appears at the beginning, but it can also appear later.

### Example

> **Reading regularly improves students' academic skills.** It increases vocabulary, develops comprehension, and exposes students to different ideas.

The first sentence is the **topic sentence**.

### Remember

> **Topic sentence → main idea of the paragraph**

But not every paragraph has an obvious topic sentence.

---

## 6. How to Find the Main Idea

Use these questions:

### Step 1 — Identify the topic

What is the paragraph about?

### Step 2 — Look for repeated ideas

Which idea appears directly or indirectly several times?

### Step 3 — Separate important ideas from examples

Examples support the main idea; they are usually not the main idea themselves.

### Step 4 — Ask:

> **What is the writer's most important message about this topic?**

### Step 5 — Summarize it in one sentence

If you can express the paragraph in one clear sentence, you probably found its main idea.

---

# 7. Finding Important Details

When looking for details, ask:

> **Who?**

> **What?**

> **Where?**

> **When?**

> **Why?**

> **How?**

### Example

> Ethiopia has a large and diverse population. Most people live in rural areas, although urban populations are growing. Addis Ababa is the country's largest urban centre.

Possible details:

* Ethiopia has a diverse population.
* Many people live in rural areas.
* Urban populations are growing.
* Addis Ababa is the largest urban centre.

---

# 8. Major vs. Minor Details

Not every detail has equal importance.

### Major details

Directly support the main idea.

### Minor details

Provide additional information about a major detail.

### Example

**Main idea:**

> Exercise improves students' health.

**Major detail:**

> Regular exercise improves cardiovascular fitness.

**Minor detail:**

> Walking, running, and cycling are examples of aerobic activities.

Think of it as:

> **Main idea → Major details → Minor details**

---

# 9. Skimming for Main Ideas

**Skimming** means reading quickly to understand the **general meaning** of a text.

When skimming, pay attention to:

* Title
* Headings
* First sentences
* Last sentences
* Repeated words
* Key terms

You do **not** need to read every word.

### Use skimming when:

* Finding the main idea
* Previewing a chapter
* Getting a general understanding
* Deciding whether a text is relevant

> **Skimming = Big picture**

Research on Ethiopian freshman Communicative English Skills courses also identifies skimming and scanning as important reading strategies, with students reporting substantial usefulness from these strategies.

---

# 10. Scanning for Details

**Scanning** means reading quickly to find **specific information**.

For example, if the question asks:

> **When did the event occur?**

Look for:

> **a date or year**

If it asks:

> **Where did it occur?**

Look for:

> **a place**

### Common targets

* Names
* Dates
* Numbers
* Places
* Facts
* Specific terms

> **Scanning = Exact information**

---

# 11. Main Idea vs. Details

| Main Idea                            | Supporting Detail                |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Central point                        | Supporting information           |
| Broad                                | More specific                    |
| Usually one major idea               | Several details                  |
| Answers “What is the writer saying?” | Answers “How/why/what evidence?” |

### Example

**Main idea:**

> Population growth creates challenges for cities.

**Details:**

* More housing is needed.
* Traffic may increase.
* Public services face greater demand.
* More jobs may be required.

---

# 12. Distinguishing Main Ideas from Examples

An example is usually **not** the main idea.

Look for signals such as:

* for example
* for instance
* such as
* including

### Example

> Ethiopia has many cultural traditions. **For example**, coffee ceremonies are important in many communities.

**Main idea:**

> Ethiopia has many cultural traditions.

**Example/detail:**

> Coffee ceremonies are important in many communities.

---

# 13. Using Signal Words

Signal words can help you recognize relationships between ideas.

### Examples

> **For example / for instance** → example

> **Because / since** → reason

> **Therefore / as a result** → result

> **However / but** → contrast

> **First / next / finally** → sequence

> **In conclusion / overall** → summary or conclusion

These words help you understand which information is central and which information supports it.

---

# 14. Main Idea Questions in Exams

Common questions include:

> **What is the main idea of the passage?**

> **What is the passage mainly about?**

> **What is the author's main point?**

> **Which statement best summarizes the passage?**

### Best strategy

Do not choose an answer simply because it contains words from the passage.

The correct answer should represent the **whole paragraph or passage**, not just one detail.

---

# 15. Detail Questions in Exams

Common questions include:

> According to the passage, why...?

> What happened when...?

> Where did...?

> When did...?

> Which of the following is mentioned?

### Best strategy

Use **scanning**.

1. Find the keyword from the question.
2. Locate the relevant part of the passage.
3. Read the surrounding sentences.
4. Select the answer supported by the text.

---

# 16. Avoiding Common Traps

### Trap 1 — Choosing a detail instead of the main idea

A choice may be true but only represent **one small part** of the passage.

### Trap 2 — Choosing something too broad

The answer may be related to the topic but not specific enough.

### Trap 3 — Choosing outside knowledge

A statement may be true in real life but **not supported by the passage**.

### Trap 4 — Choosing an attractive but unsupported answer

Always ask:

> **Where is the evidence in the text?**

---

# 17. A Simple Reading Method

Use:

> **T → M → D → S**

### **T — Topic**

Identify what the text is about.

### **M — Main Idea**

Identify the central message.

### **D — Details**

Find information supporting the main idea.

### **S — Summarize**

Express the main idea and most important details briefly.

### Example

**Topic:** Exercise

**Main idea:** Exercise improves students' physical and mental well-being.

**Details:**

* Improves fitness
* Reduces stress
* Improves sleep

**Summary:**

> Regular exercise supports students' physical and mental well-being.

---

# 18. Reading Under Exam Conditions

When time is limited:

### First — Read the questions

Know what information you need.

### Second — Skim the passage

Identify its topic and general structure.

### Third — Find the main idea

Look for the central message.

### Fourth — Scan for details

Search for names, dates, numbers, and specific facts.

### Fifth — Check the evidence

Make sure your answer is supported by the passage.

> **Question → Keyword → Locate → Read → Answer**

---

## ⭐ Exam Focus

### Memorize these distinctions:

> **Main idea = central message**

> **Supporting details = information that develops the main idea**

> **Topic = general subject**

> **Topic sentence = sentence that often expresses the main idea**

> **Skimming = general idea**

> **Scanning = specific details**

### Main idea shortcut

Ask:

> **“If I could keep only ONE idea from this paragraph, what would it be?”**

That is usually the **main idea**.

### Detail shortcut

Ask:

> **“What information does the writer give to explain or prove the main idea?”**

Those are the **supporting details**.

## Quick Check

### 1. What is the main idea?

> Exercise helps students stay healthy. It strengthens the body, reduces stress, and can improve sleep.

**Answer:** Exercise helps students stay healthy.

---

### 2. Which is a supporting detail?

> Exercise helps students stay healthy. It strengthens the body, reduces stress, and can improve sleep.

**Answer:** It can reduce stress.

---

### 3. What is the difference between skimming and scanning?

**Answer:** **Skimming** is used to understand the general idea; **scanning** is used to find specific information.

---

### 4. What question helps you identify the main idea?

**Answer:** “What is the writer mainly saying?”

---

### 5. What is a topic?

**Answer:** The general subject being discussed.

---

### 6. What is a topic sentence?

**Answer:** A sentence that usually states or introduces the main idea of a paragraph.

---

### 7. Why should you not choose an answer simply because it appears in the passage?

**Answer:** Because it may be only a supporting detail rather than the main idea.

---

### 8. If a question asks “When?”, what should you scan for?

**Answer:** A **date, year, or time**.
