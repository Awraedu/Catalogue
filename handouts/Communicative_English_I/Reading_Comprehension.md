# Reading Comprehension

> **Core idea:** **Reading comprehension** is the ability to read a text, understand its meaning, identify important information, and make logical conclusions from what you read.

## 1. What Is Reading Comprehension?

Reading comprehension is more than simply reading words.

A good reader should be able to:

* Understand the **main idea**
* Find **specific information**
* Identify **supporting details**
* Understand unfamiliar words from **context**
* Recognize the writer's purpose
* Make **inferences**
* Distinguish facts from opinions
* Summarize the text

> **Read → Understand → Connect → Interpret**

The Ethiopian freshman English course places reading comprehension within its goal of developing students' ability to understand academic and other texts in English.

---

# 2. Before Reading

Good comprehension often begins **before** you read the whole text.

### Preview the text

Look at:

* Title
* Headings
* Pictures
* Graphs
* First sentences
* Key words

Then ask:

> **What is this text probably about?**

### Predict

Use the information you already have to predict the content.

For example:

**Title:** *Population Density in Ethiopia*

You can predict that the text may discuss:

* Population
* Cities and rural areas
* Crowded areas
* Population distribution
* Causes of differences in density

> **Prediction gives your reading a purpose.**

---

# 3. Reading for the Main Idea

The **main idea** is the central point of a paragraph or text.

Ask:

> **What is the writer mainly talking about?**

### Example

> Many students find university life difficult at first. They must manage their time, attend lectures, complete assignments, and study independently. Good time management can help students adapt successfully.

### Main idea:

> **Good time management helps students adapt to university life.**

The other sentences provide **supporting information**.

---

# 4. Supporting Details

Supporting details explain, prove, or develop the main idea.

They may include:

* Examples
* Facts
* Reasons
* Explanations
* Descriptions
* Statistics

### Example

**Main idea:**

> Exercise benefits university students.

**Supporting details:**

* It improves physical fitness.
* It can improve concentration.
* It can reduce stress.
* It can improve sleep.

> **Main idea = central point**
> **Details = information supporting the point**

---

# 5. Topic vs. Main Idea

These are not the same.

### Topic

The **general subject** of a text or paragraph.

> **Topic:** Population growth

### Main idea

What the writer says about that topic.

> **Main idea:** Rapid population growth can increase pressure on housing, education, healthcare, and other services.

### Easy trick

> **Topic = What?**

> **Main idea = What about it?**

---

# 6. Skimming

**Skimming** means reading quickly to understand the **general idea** of a text.

You do not read every word.

Look at:

* Title
* Headings
* First sentences
* Key words
* Conclusions

### Use skimming when:

* You want the main idea.
* You want to understand the general topic.
* You need to decide whether a text is relevant.
* You have limited time.

> **Skim = Get the big picture.**

---

# 7. Scanning

**Scanning** means reading quickly to find **specific information**.

You search for a particular:

* Name
* Date
* Number
* Place
* Word
* Fact

### Example

Question:

> **When was the population census conducted?**

Do not read the entire passage slowly.

Scan for:

> **a year or date**

> **Skimming = general idea**
> **Scanning = specific information**

---

# 8. Reading for Specific Information

When a question asks for a particular fact, identify the **keyword** in the question.

### Example

Question:

> Where does the largest population live?

Keyword:

> **Where**

Then search the passage for information about **location**.

### Common question words

| Question      | What to look for  |
| ------------- | ----------------- |
| **Who?**      | Person/group      |
| **What?**     | Thing/action/idea |
| **Where?**    | Place             |
| **When?**     | Time/date         |
| **Why?**      | Reason/cause      |
| **How?**      | Method/process    |
| **How many?** | Number            |
| **Which?**    | Choice/category   |

---

# 9. Context Clues

Sometimes you will find a word you do not know.

Do not immediately stop and use a dictionary.

Use the **context**.

Context clues are surrounding words and sentences that help you understand an unfamiliar word.

### Example

> The area is **arid**. It receives very little rainfall, and water is scarce.

Even if you do not know *arid*, the context suggests:

> **arid = very dry**

### Common context clues

* Definition
* Example
* Synonym
* Antonym
* Explanation
* Cause and effect

> **Unknown word + surrounding information = possible meaning**

---

# 10. Making Inferences

An **inference** is a conclusion you reach from information that is **not directly stated**.

### Example

> The streets were wet, and people were carrying umbrellas.

The text does not directly say:

> “It was raining.”

But you can infer:

> **It was probably raining.**

### Remember

> **Inference = Evidence + Reasoning**

Do not make an inference without evidence from the text.

---

# 11. Fact vs. Opinion

### Fact

A statement that can be supported or verified.

> Ethiopia has many different cultural groups.

### Opinion

A personal belief, judgment, or feeling.

> Ethiopia has the most beautiful culture in Africa.

Words that may signal opinions:

* I think
* I believe
* should
* best
* beautiful
* terrible
* excellent

> **Fact = can be verified**
> **Opinion = belief or judgment**

---

# 12. Identifying the Writer's Purpose

Ask:

> **Why did the writer write this text?**

Common purposes include:

### To inform

> Give facts or information.

### To explain

> Make something easier to understand.

### To describe

> Give details about a person, place, thing, or situation.

### To persuade

> Convince the reader to accept an idea.

### To entertain

> Provide enjoyment.

### Example

A text explaining the causes of population growth is probably written mainly:

> **To inform or explain.**

---

# 13. Understanding Paragraph Organization

A paragraph often follows a logical structure.

### Common patterns

**Description**

> Describes a person, place, thing, or situation.

**Sequence**

> Presents events or steps in order.

**Cause and effect**

> Explains why something happens and its results.

**Compare and contrast**

> Shows similarities and differences.

**Problem and solution**

> Presents a problem and possible solutions.

Recognizing the structure helps you understand the writer's ideas more quickly.

---

# 14. Signal Words

Signal words show relationships between ideas.

### Addition

* also
* furthermore
* moreover
* in addition

### Contrast

* however
* but
* although
* in contrast
* on the other hand

### Cause

* because
* since
* due to
* because of

### Effect

* therefore
* as a result
* consequently
* thus

### Example

* for example
* for instance
* such as

### Sequence

* first
* next
* then
* finally

> **Signal words are clues to the organization of the text.**

# 15. Taking Notes While Reading

The Ethiopian FLEn 1011 module explicitly encourages students to **make notes while reading**.

Do not copy the entire text.

Write:

* Main ideas
* Key terms
* Important facts
* Examples
* Causes
* Effects
* Dates/numbers
* Conclusions

### Example

Instead of writing:

> “Population density refers to the number of people living in a particular unit of land area...”

Write:

> **Population density = people per unit of land**

Good notes are:

> **Short + clear + useful**

# 16. Answering Comprehension Questions

Use this method:

### Step 1 — Read the question

Understand exactly what it asks.

### Step 2 — Identify keywords

Find the important words.

### Step 3 — Locate the information

Scan the passage.

### Step 4 — Read around the answer

Do not rely only on one sentence. Check the surrounding context.

### Step 5 — Eliminate wrong answers

For multiple-choice questions, remove answers that:

* Contradict the text
* Are not mentioned
* Are too broad
* Are too narrow
* Use incorrect information

### Step 6 — Check the evidence

Ask:

> **Can I support my answer from the text?**

---

# 17. Common Types of Comprehension Questions

### Main idea questions

> What is the passage mainly about?

### Detail questions

> According to the passage, what caused...?

### Vocabulary questions

> What does the word “...” mean in the passage?

### Reference questions

> What does **“it”** refer to?

### Inference questions

> What can be inferred from the passage?

### Purpose questions

> Why did the writer mention...?

### Organization questions

> How is the information organized?

---

# 18. A Powerful Reading Strategy

Use:

> **P → S → R → Q → C**

### **P — Preview**

Look at the title, headings, and visuals.

### **S — Skim**

Find the general idea.

### **R — Read**

Read carefully for important information.

### **Q — Question**

Ask what the writer is saying and why.

### **C — Check**

Check your understanding and answers.

---

# 19. Reading Comprehension Under Exam Conditions

You do not always need to read every word slowly.

### First

**Read the questions quickly.**

Know what information you need.

### Then

**Skim the passage.**

Understand its general structure.

### Next

**Scan for keywords.**

Locate relevant sections.

### Finally

**Read carefully around the answer.**

> **Question → Keyword → Locate → Read → Answer**

This is especially useful when time is limited.

## Quick Check

1. What is reading comprehension?
   **Answer:** The ability to understand, interpret, and use information from a text.

2. What is the difference between skimming and scanning?
   **Answer:** Skimming finds the general idea; scanning finds specific information.

3. What is the main idea?
   **Answer:** The central point of a text or paragraph.

4. What are supporting details?
   **Answer:** Information that explains, develops, or supports the main idea.

5. What is an inference?
   **Answer:** A conclusion reached from evidence and reasoning.

6. What should you do when you meet an unfamiliar word?
   **Answer:** Use surrounding words and sentences as context clues before relying on a dictionary.

7. What is the purpose of taking notes while reading?
   **Answer:** To record and organize the most important information.

8. If a question asks **“When?”**, what information should you search for?
   **Answer:** A time, date, year, or period.

9. Which strategy is best for finding a specific name in a long passage?
   **Answer:** **Scanning.**

10. Which strategy is best for understanding what an entire passage is about?
    **Answer:** **Skimming.**
