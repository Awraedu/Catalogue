# Using Lecturer Cues and Identifying Main Ideas

> **Core idea:** Good lecture listening means recognizing **signals from the lecturer**, identifying the **main ideas**, and separating them from supporting details and examples.

## 1. What Are Lecturer Cues?

**Lecturer cues** are words, phrases, changes in voice, repetition, gestures, or other signals that help students recognize important information.

Lecturers use cues to show:

* A main idea
* A new section
* An example
* A sequence
* A contrast
* A cause or result
* A conclusion

### Example

> **“There are three main causes of population growth.”**

This tells you that:

* A new topic is beginning.
* There are **three main points**.
* You should listen carefully for each one.

---

# 2. Why Are Lecturer Cues Important?

A lecture can contain a large amount of information.

Not everything is equally important.

Cues help you decide:

> **What should I write down?**

> **What is the main point?**

> **What is only an example?**

> **When does a new section begin?**

The Ethiopian FLEn 1011 course specifically develops listening skills such as recognizing **discourse markers**, noticing the **structure of a lecture**, and following a speaker's organization.

---

# 3. Cues for Main Ideas

Lecturers often introduce important ideas with phrases such as:

> **The main point is...**

> **The most important thing is...**

> **The key idea is...**

> **The main reason is...**

> **It is important to remember that...**

> **One important factor is...**

### Example

> “**The main reason for population growth is natural increase.**”

### Note:

> **Main cause → natural increase**

You do not need to write the entire sentence.

---

# 4. Cues for New Topics

These phrases often signal a change to a new section:

> **Now let's look at...**

> **Next, we will discuss...**

> **Let's move on to...**

> **The next point is...**

> **Another important issue is...**

> **Now I would like to focus on...**

### Example

> “Now let's move on to the **effects of population growth**.”

Your notes might become:

> **2. Effects of population growth**

This creates a clear structure.

---

# 5. Listing Cues

Lecturers often list several points.

Common signals include:

> **First...**

> **First of all...**

> **Second...**

> **Third...**

> **Finally...**

> **There are three main reasons...**

> **The first factor is...**

> **Another factor is...**

### Example

> “There are three major causes. **First**, economic conditions. **Second**, education. **Finally**, employment opportunities.”

### Notes:

> **Causes:**
>
> 1. Economic conditions
> 2. Education
> 3. Employment

---

# 6. Sequencing Cues

**Sequencing** means showing the order in which ideas or events occur.

Useful expressions:

> **First**

> **Then**

> **Next**

> **After that**

> **Following this**

> **Finally**

### Example

> First, the population is measured. **Next**, the data are analyzed. **Finally**, the results are reported.

### Notes:

> **1. Measure → 2. Analyze → 3. Report**

---

# 7. Cues for Examples

Examples support a main idea.

Listen for:

> **For example...**

> **For instance...**

> **Such as...**

> **To illustrate...**

> **An example of this is...**

### Example

> “Many factors affect population distribution. **For example,** climate can influence where people live.”

### Notes:

> **Population distribution → many factors**
> Example: **climate**

The main idea is more important than the example.

---

# 8. Cues for Explanation

Sometimes a lecturer explains or clarifies an idea.

Listen for:

> **In other words...**

> **This means...**

> **That is...**

> **To put it another way...**

> **What I mean is...**

### Example

> “Population density is the number of people per unit area. **In other words**, it tells us how crowded a particular area is.”

The second sentence explains the first.

---

# 9. Cues for Contrast

These indicate that two ideas are different.

Common cues:

> **However...**

> **But...**

> **On the other hand...**

> **In contrast...**

> **Unlike...**

> **Whereas...**

### Example

> “Urban areas usually have high population density. **In contrast**, some rural areas have very low population density.”

### Notes:

> **Urban → high density**
> **Rural → low density**

---

# 10. Cues for Cause and Effect

These show relationships between events or ideas.

### Cause signals

> **Because...**

> **Since...**

> **Due to...**

> **As a result of...**

### Effect signals

> **Therefore...**

> **As a result...**

> **Consequently...**

> **Thus...**

### Example

> “Many people move to cities **because** of employment opportunities. **As a result**, urban populations increase.”

### Notes:

> **Jobs → migration to cities → ↑ urban population**

---

# 11. Cues for Emphasis

Sometimes the lecturer wants you to pay special attention.

Listen for:

> **Most importantly...**

> **Remember that...**

> **Pay attention to...**

> **It is important to note...**

> **Especially...**

> **Above all...**

> **This is particularly important...**

### Example

> “**Remember that** population density and population distribution are not the same thing.”

### Note:

> ★ **Density ≠ Distribution**

---

# 12. Cues for Repetition

If a lecturer repeats something, it may be important.

### Example

> “Population density is important. **Again, population density is the number of people per unit area.**”

This repetition is a strong signal.

### Strategy

When you hear an idea repeated:

> **★ Mark it as important.**

---

# 13. Cues for Definitions

Definitions are especially important in academic lectures.

Listen for:

> **X is...**

> **X refers to...**

> **X means...**

> **X can be defined as...**

> **By X, we mean...**

### Example

> “Population distribution **refers to the way people are spread across an area**.”

### Note:

> **Population distribution = how people are spread across an area**

---

# 14. Cues for Conclusions

Lecturers often signal the end of a section or lecture.

Common expressions:

> **In conclusion...**

> **To conclude...**

> **To summarize...**

> **In summary...**

> **Overall...**

> **The main points are...**

> **Finally...**

### Example

> “**To summarize**, population distribution is influenced by physical, economic, and social factors.”

### Strategy

Listen carefully at the end because the lecturer may repeat the most important ideas.

---

# 15. Identifying the Main Idea

The **main idea** is the central point the speaker wants you to understand.

Ask:

> **What is the lecturer mainly talking about?**

### Example

Lecture:

> “Exercise improves cardiovascular health. It strengthens muscles and bones, improves mood, and can also improve sleep.”

### Main idea:

> **Exercise has many health benefits.**

### Supporting details:

* Improves cardiovascular health
* Strengthens muscles and bones
* Improves mood
* Improves sleep

---

# 16. Main Idea vs Supporting Detail

This distinction is extremely important.

| Main idea                            | Supporting detail                  |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Central message                      | Information explaining the message |
| Broad                                | More specific                      |
| Most important                       | Less important                     |
| Answers “What is this mainly about?” | Answers “How? Why? For example?”   |

### Example

> **Main idea:** Exercise improves physical and mental health.

> **Detail:** It can improve sleep.

> **Detail:** It can strengthen muscles.

> **Example:** Walking regularly can improve cardiovascular fitness.

---

# 17. Main Idea vs Example

Do not mistake an example for the main idea.

### Lecturer:

> “Wildlife tourism provides economic benefits. **For example, national parks create jobs for guides and hotel workers.**”

### Main idea:

> **Wildlife tourism provides economic benefits.**

### Example:

> Jobs for guides and hotel workers.

### Signal:

> **For example**

This tells you that the information that follows is supporting information.

---

# 18. Listen for the Structure

A well-organized lecture often follows:

> **Introduction → Main Point 1 → Main Point 2 → Main Point 3 → Conclusion**

### Example

**Introduction**

> Population distribution is influenced by several factors.

**Point 1**

> Physical factors.

**Point 2**

> Economic factors.

**Point 3**

> Social factors.

**Conclusion**

> These factors interact to determine where people live.

### Notes:

> **Population distribution**
> → Physical
> → Economic
> → Social
> → Conclusion

# 20. A Practical Listening Method

Use **CUE → IDEA → DETAIL**.

### 1. CUE

Listen for a signal.

> “The first reason is...”

### 2. IDEA

Identify the main point.

> **First reason → employment**

### 3. DETAIL

Record useful supporting information.

> **Jobs attract people to cities.**

### Result:

> **1. Employment**
> → Jobs attract migrants to cities.

---

# 21. Don't Treat Every Sentence Equally

During a lecture:

### ⭐ High priority

* Definitions
* Main ideas
* Lecturer emphasis
* Lists
* Conclusions
* Formulas
* Important facts

### ◼ Medium priority

* Supporting explanations
* Useful details
* Comparisons

### ○ Lower priority

* Repeated obvious information
* Long examples that do not add new information
* Unnecessary conversational comments

> **Your goal is not to capture everything. Your goal is to capture what matters.**

# 22. Example Lecture

Imagine the lecturer says:

> “Today we will discuss population density. **First**, we will define population density. **Second**, we will look at how it is calculated. **Finally**, we will examine factors affecting population density. **For example**, climate and economic opportunities can influence where people live. **Remember**, population density is not the same as population distribution. **In conclusion**, population density measures the number of people living in a particular area.”

### Good notes:

> **Population Density**
>
> **1. Definition**
> → people per unit area
>
> **2. Calculation**
>
> **3. Factors**
> → climate
> → economic opportunities
>
> ★ **Density ≠ distribution**
>
> **Conclusion:** number of people per area

Notice that the notes are much shorter than the lecture.

# 23. Common Mistakes

### ❌ Writing every sentence

You may miss the next important idea.

### ✅ Identify the idea and record keywords.

---

### ❌ Treating examples as main ideas

### ✅ Look for signals such as **for example** and **for instance**.

---

### ❌ Ignoring transition words

Words such as **first, next, however, therefore, finally** reveal the structure.

### ✅ Circle or underline them in your notes.

---

### ❌ Focusing only on the beginning

Important information may appear throughout the lecture and especially in the conclusion.

### ✅ Listen continuously.

---

### ❌ Copying the lecturer's exact words

### ✅ Paraphrase using short keywords.

| -------------------------- | ------------------ |
| **First, second, finally** | Listing/sequence   |

### The golden rule

> **Cue → Identify the function → Find the idea → Note the keywords**

# Quick Check

### 1. What is a lecturer cue?

**Answer:** A word, phrase, gesture, emphasis, or other signal that helps identify important information or the structure of a lecture.

### 2. What does “first, second, finally” signal?

**Answer:** A list or sequence of points.

### 3. What does “for example” usually introduce?

**Answer:** A supporting example.

### 4. What does “however” signal?

**Answer:** Contrast or a different idea.

### 5. What does “therefore” usually signal?

**Answer:** A result or conclusion.

### 6. What is the main idea?

**Answer:** The central or most important message of a lecture or section.

### 7. Identify the main idea:

> “Exercise improves sleep. It can help people fall asleep more easily and improve sleep quality.”

**Answer:** **Exercise can improve sleep.**

### 8. Identify the supporting detail:

> “Wildlife tourism creates employment. For example, it provides jobs for guides.”

**Answer:** **It provides jobs for guides.**

### 9. What should you do when you hear “Remember that...” ?

**Answer:** Pay special attention because the lecturer is signaling important information.

### 10. Complete:

> **Cue → ______ → Detail**

**Answer:** **Main idea**
